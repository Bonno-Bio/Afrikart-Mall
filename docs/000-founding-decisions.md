# Afrikart Mall — Founding Decisions Memo v0.1

**Status:** For discussion. No spending decisions to be made from this document.
**Author:** Prepared as a strategic response to the Afrikart Mall concept brief.
**Date:** November 2025

---

## 0. What this document is, and what it is not

It is **not** a business plan, a brand book, or a 15-chapter blueprint. Those are documents you write
*after* you know which business you are in.

It **is** an attempt to answer the four questions that decide whether Afrikart becomes a company or a
website:

1. Who actually holds the transaction?
2. Who owns the last mile?
3. What is the average basket, and can it carry the cost of delivery?
4. Which customer wallet are we chasing while household spending is shrinking?

Everything else — pillars, stages, org charts, AI agents — is downstream of those four.

The brief's instinct is right: **think like an empire from day one.** But empires are not built by
committing to seven pillars early. They are built by winning one narrow, defensible position and
then expanding outward from strength. The brief already says the right thing — "each pillar
strengthens the others" — but that flywheel only spins if the first pillar has positive unit
economics. If it does not, more pillars just multiply the loss.

## 1. The four truths from Botswana's own graveyard

We do not have to guess what kills e-commerce in this market. It has already happened, repeatedly,
in the last three years, and the post-mortems are public.

### Truth 1 — Disintermediation is the #1 killer, and it is invisible

At least five e-commerce startups in Botswana have closed since 2023. The most instructive is
**Artify Botswana**, an art marketplace that shut down in early 2024. Its finding: buyers browsed the
site, then completed the purchase **offline** — directly with the seller. Because the platform ran a
commission model, it earned nothing on the transactions it had generated.

> *Source: TechCabal, "Logistics and payments hiccups throttling Botswana's e-commerce ambitions," 26 Sep 2024 — [techcabal.com/2024/09/26/botswana-ecommerce](https://techcabal.com/2024/09/26/botswana-ecommerce/)*

This is the most important paragraph in this memo. In a market of 2.4 million people where commerce
already runs on WhatsApp, a listing platform has **no control over the transaction**. If the buyer and
seller can finish the deal in a DM, they will — because it saves them both money. A commission-only
marketplace in Botswana is not a business, it is a free lead-generation service for its sellers.

**Implication:** Afrikart must hold at least one of: the money, the goods, or the delivery. Ideally
all three in the categories that matter. If we are a catalogue, we lose.

### Truth 2 — The last mile is the business, and per-parcel couriers make it impossible

**Vaisa**, a Botswana delivery/e-commerce venture, was defeated by last-mile logistics. Its
co-founder's conclusion was blunt: *"The best way to handle logistics in Botswana is to have an
in-house fleet because courier companies would be too expensive. But having your fleet that can
deliver nationwide is another challenge on its own."*

Independent commentary on Botswana e-commerce reaches the same conclusion: the digital layer is
ready, and the **last mile is the actual constraint** — geography, no reliable address system,
inconsistent delivery, and no-show drivers eroding trust.

> *Sources: TechCabal (as above); Afrikan Insights, "E-Commerce Growth in Botswana: Ready, Not Ready?" Aug 2025.*

**Implication:** Afrikart is not a retail technology company that has a delivery problem. It is a
delivery company that sells goods. We should sequence accordingly.

### Truth 3 — The wallet is shrinking, so discretionary categories are a trap

Botswana retail is roughly **29% of GDP** and is dominated by South African chains and Choppies.
Household behaviour in 2025 is defensive: **53% of consumers reduced discretionary spending in
Q2 2025, and 57% planned further reductions**, driven by weakness in the diamond sector.

> *Source: USDA FAS, Botswana Exporter Guide 2025.*

**Implication:** A launch catalogue built on televisions, furniture and consumer electronics is
launching into a market that has stopped buying those things, and where the price benchmark is
already set by Game, HiFi Corp and Choppies. Meanwhile these are exactly the categories with the
worst economics: low margin, high damage rate, warranty returns, and a delivery cost that can exceed
20% of the item's value.

### Truth 4 — Half of Botswana's online spend already leaks out of the country

From the brief's own data: **48.4% of e-commerce users bought from national sellers, and 34% of
purchases went to Asian sellers.** Roughly half the online wallet leaves the country.

This reframes the competitive question entirely. The enemy is not another Botswana website. The
enemy is **Shein, Temu, AliExpress and Amazon** — and their weakness in this market is precise and
exploitable:

| Their weakness | Our counter |
|---|---|
| 2–4 week delivery | Stocked locally, delivered in 24–48 hours |
| No real returns process | Returns at the door, no shipping back to Shenzhen |
| Customs duty surprises | Price at checkout is the price paid |
| No local warranty or recourse | Local entity, local dispute resolution |
| No WhatsApp support | WhatsApp *is* the support channel |

We do not need to beat them on price. **We beat them on arrival time, certainty and returns** — and
that is worth a price premium in a market where a P400 order arriving wrong is a genuine loss to the
household.

### The cautionary tale for the "empire" ambition

Two data points should govern how fast we scale:

- **Copia Global** raised **over $120 million** to digitise rural commerce in Kenya through a 50,000-agent
  network. It entered administration in May 2024 and was ordered into liquidation; at bankruptcy it
  held roughly **$20,300 in cash against $45.5 million in liabilities**. It solved a real access
  problem and still produced weak commercial economics.
- **Jumia** — the company we are notionally emulating — exited **South Africa and Tunisia in 2024** and
  **Algeria in 2026**, because those markets' competitive and macroeconomic conditions could not
  support the unit economics. Even Jumia has concluded that geographic breadth is a liability until
  the unit economics work.

> *Sources: Launch Base Africa (Copia liquidation, Jul & Nov 2024); SEC filing 16 Oct 2024 (Jumia market exits).*

**Implication:** Reaching underserved customers creates enormous social value and can still be
commercially fatal. Expansion must replicate a **proven P&L**, never a proven brand.

## 2. The four structural decisions

These are not in the brief's 15 areas. They should be.

### D1. The transaction must be held by us, in at least one category

| Model | What we control | Can it survive in Botswana? |
|---|---|---|
| Pure listing / commission | Nothing | **No.** Artify's exact failure mode. |
| Managed marketplace (curated sellers, we take payment + deliver) | Money, delivery, experience | **Yes** — take rate 10–15% |
| Consignment (seller's stock, we hold and ship it) | Money, goods, delivery | **Yes** — 20–28% effective margin |
| Owned inventory (we buy, we sell) | Everything | **Yes** — 28–35% margin |
| Commission + delivery only | Delivery + payment | Marginal; only works with high volume and high AOV |

The strategic insight: **a 5–8% commission cannot fund a last mile that costs 15–20% of the basket.**
The commission model is arithmetically incompatible with the delivery reality of this market. Either
the take rate goes up because we do more work (managed/consignment), or the model dies.

### D2. Own the last mile in one city before buying a single national courier contract

Test: a Gaborone order, delivered by our own rider on a batched suburb route.

- **Owned rider, batched:** ~18–20 drops/day at ~P320 all-in daily cost (rider + scooter + fuel + insurance) ≈ **P16–P18 per drop**.
- **Per-parcel courier:** **P60–P80 per parcel** inside Gaborone.

That gap is the difference between a viable business and a subsidy. It is also the reason to keep the
delivery zone deliberately small: **density is the product.** Ten drops in Extension 12 cost almost
nothing extra; ten drops spread across Gaborone, Tlokweng and Mogoditshane do not.

For national reach, do not build. Rent: **PAXI, BotswanaPost, Sprint Couriers, Aramex**, and the
intercity combi/bus networks that already move parcels to villages. Pay per parcel, no capex, no fleet
risk.

### D3. Average order value is the master variable — sell baskets, not items

This is the single most under-rated line in the whole brief. Every cost below the basket line is
roughly fixed per delivery. So margin is driven almost entirely by *what is in the basket*, not by
how many visitors the site gets.

From the model in `model/afrikart-unit-economics.xlsx` (AOV P380, 30% product margin, P30 delivery fee):

| | Per order |
|---|---|
| Product gross profit | P114.00 |
| Delivery fee collected | P30.00 |
| **Revenue per order** | **P144.00** |
| Last mile | (P18.00) |
| Pick, pack, packaging | (P15.00) |
| Payment processing (2.5%) | (P9.50) |
| Returns & breakage (4%) | (P15.20) |
| Customer support (3%) | (P11.40) |
| Marketing / CAC | (P20.00) |
| **Contribution margin** | **P54.90** (14.4% of AOV) |
| Fixed costs / month | P30,500 |
| **Break-even** | **556 orders/month ≈ 21 orders/day** |

Twenty-one orders a day. That is the real launch target — not "50 merchants signed," not "10,000
Instagram followers." A P2.5m annual GMV business breaks even in Gaborone. That is achievable and it
is honest.

But look at what happens when the basket collapses to a single P220 accessory:

| AOV | Orders/day needed to break even |
|---|---|
| P220 | ~P1,300+ orders/month — **not reachable on a single-item basket** |
| P380 | ~21/day |
| P650 | ~13/day |

**Cheap single-item orders delivered one at a time is precisely the configuration that has killed
every Botswana marketplace so far.** The remedy is commercial, not technical: bundles (nappy + wipes +
bum cream), minimum order values, free delivery above a threshold, and category adjacency in the
recommendation engine.

### D4. Launch into non-discretionary spend only

Given the consumer squeeze, the launch catalogue should be: **high frequency, non-perishable,
non-discretionary, small parcel, high margin, repeat purchase.** That narrows it sharply:

| Category | Why it works | Why it's risky |
|---|---|---|
| **Baby & mother** (nappies, formula, wipes) | Weekly repurchase, price-predictable, high trust value | Price-sensitive; must not be beaten on shelf price |
| **Personal care & hair** (wigs, weaves, extensions, skincare) | Very high margin, 4–8 week repurchase, WhatsApp-native, small parcel, import-heavy so local stock wins | Supplier quality control; authenticity |
| **Household consumables** (cleaning, paper, kitchen) | Predictable, bundled easily | Bulky; watch cubic weight |
| **Phone accessories** (chargers, cases, power banks) | Tiny parcels, high margin, universal demand | Low AOV alone — must bundle |
| ~~Groceries~~ | — | Thin margin, perishable, Choppies war, wastage |
| ~~Furniture / TVs / large appliances~~ | — | Delivery cost, damage, warranty, price comparison vs SA chains, discretionary spend collapsed |

Pick **one** of the first four to start. My recommendation is **baby & mother plus hair/beauty** as the
opening pair: they share a customer (the household's primary shopper, usually a woman), they bundle
naturally, and together they lift AOV without adding delivery stops.

## 3. Founding Decision 001 — the recommendation

The brief's options were A (pure marketplace), B (traditional retailer), C (hybrid). My answer is
**C, but with an important inversion.**

### Recommendation: C′ — "The Owned Marketplace"

> We launch as a **curated, managed marketplace**: a small number of verified suppliers, but we hold
> the payment, the delivery, the returns promise and the customer relationship. We start with
> consignment and selected owned stock; we open to self-service third-party sellers only once the
> delivery network reliably works.

What that means concretely:

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Do we own stock? | **Some** — the fast movers, consignment first, owned where margin justifies it |
| Do we take payment? | **Always.** No offline completion, no "pay the seller directly" |
| Do we deliver? | **Always, in Gaborone**, in-house. Rented for the rest of the country |
| Do we list every seller? | **No.** 5–8 suppliers, curated, in one category, at launch |
| Do we take commission? | Yes, but as a **managed** rate (10–15%), plus delivery, not a 5% toll |
| When do we become a real marketplace? | When delivery works and a seller would be stupid not to join |

Why not pure A: it hands the transaction to the seller, which is exactly how Artify died.
Why not pure B: it caps us as a small retailer with all the inventory risk and none of the network
effects — no flywheel, no empire.
Why C′: it captures the margin needed to fund delivery *and* builds the two assets that compound —
the delivery route network and the seller base.

### The one-line positioning

> **"Afrikart gets it to your door in Gaborone tomorrow, and if it's wrong we take it back."**

Not "Botswana's biggest online store." Not "everything for everyone." The promise is **certainty**,
because certainty is the thing the market has repeatedly failed to deliver, and the thing foreign
sellers structurally cannot offer.

## 4. Founding Decision 002 — capital

**Recommendation: Lean, with a hard milestone gate.** Not because ambition is wrong, but because in
this market more capital applied to an unproven unit economics simply loses money faster — that is
the Copia lesson at 120 million dollars.

The pilot as modelled needs roughly:

| Item | Monthly | 90-day total |
|---|---|---|
| Storage unit (60–80 sqm industrial) | P6,000 | P18,000 |
| Team (picker/packer + ops/support) | P16,000 | P48,000 |
| Tech, hosting, tools | P3,000 | P9,000 |
| Admin, compliance, insurance | P5,500 | P16,500 |
| Marketing | P12,000 | P36,000 |
| Working capital (stock float) | — | P60,000–P100,000 |
| Contingency (20%) | — | P30,000 |
| **Total 90-day requirement** | | **≈ P220,000–P260,000** |

That is a number two founders can raise locally without dilution. And there is a Botswana-specific
advantage most foreign competitors do not have: **CEDA, the Botswana Development Corporation, the
Youth Development Fund and commercial bank SME facilities** are sources of patient, largely
non-dilutive capital for citizen-owned businesses. We should map eligibility early — but *after* the
pilot produces a P&L worth showing them, because that is what turns an application into an approval.

**What I would explicitly refuse:** raising venture capital to fund a national fleet and a fulfilment
centre before Gaborone is contribution-positive. That is the Copia/Jumia playbook and it has a known
ending.

## 5. Founding Decision 003 — launch ambition

**Recommendation: Gaborone pilot, national reach by rented carriers, designed for Southern Africa
from day one.**

Meaning:

- **Operate** in Gaborone and (optionally) Mogoditshane/Tlokweng only. Owned delivery, batched routes.
- **Sell** nationally from day one via PAXI/BotswanaPost/combi networks — but only for products whose
  margin survives a P60–P80 intercity parcel cost. Most will not; that is fine, and it is a filter,
  not a failure.
- **Design** the data model, tax logic and currency handling for multi-country from the start
  (cheap now, expensive later), but do not open a second market until Botswana is profitable.

A note on the regional play that the brief does not exploit: **Botswana sits inside SACU.** Goods
move duty-free between Botswana, South Africa, Namibia, Lesotho and Eswatini, and Gaborone is a
few hours' truck ride from Gauteng — the densest wholesale and 3PL supply chain on the continent.
That is a structural sourcing advantage Kenya and Nigeria do not have. Our supply chain should lean
on it hard, and Namibia (small, sparse, similar income profile, same customs union) is a far more
sensible second market than South Africa, where we would be attacking Takealot on its home turf.

## 6. What to do in the next 90 days instead of writing 15 chapters

The 15-area blueprint is a good table of contents. It is a bad first task. Most of those chapters
cannot be written honestly until we have run the pilot — market research, product strategy, seller
ecosystem, customer experience, logistics, financial model and marketing are all *empirical* in this
market. Only four decisions genuinely block launch:

| # | Needs deciding now | Why it blocks everything |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Business model (C′) | Determines revenue, margin, and who holds the transaction |
| 2 | Launch category + the 5–8 suppliers | Determines AOV, margin and delivery profile |
| 3 | Unit economics + break-even target | Determines whether the business can exist at all |
| 4 | Compliance basics (entity, tax, data, consumer, payment provider) | Determines whether we can legally take money |

Everything else is learned, not planned.

### The 90-day plan

**Days 1–15 — Ground truth, not desk research**
- Interview 20 households who already buy online (what they bought, what they paid, what went wrong).
- Interview 15 suppliers in the chosen category: current wholesale price, minimum order, terms, who else sells them.
- Mystery-shop the incumbents: place 5 real orders with local competitors and 2 with foreign sellers. Time every delivery. Record every failure.
- Get three courier quotes and three intercity quotes. Get two commercial rent quotes.
- Confirm the actual landed cost of our top 30 SKUs, including duty and freight.

**Days 16–40 — Buy the last mile before buying the software**
- Contract one 60–80 sqm unit. Hire one rider, one picker/packer.
- Launch with **WhatsApp + a hosted storefront** (Shopify/WooCommerce-grade, not custom-built). The goal of month 1 is to prove delivery, not to prove design.
- 50 orders/day capacity, one suburb route per day. Measure: drops per route hour, failed delivery rate, first-attempt success rate.

**Days 41–70 — Prove the basket**
- Introduce bundles, minimum order value, free-delivery threshold.
- Track AOV weekly. If AOV will not move above P300, the category is wrong — change the category before changing anything else.
- Build the seller pitch from evidence: "here are 200 orders we delivered last month."

**Days 71–90 — Decide**
- Rebuild the model with real numbers. Either the contribution margin is positive and the route density is improving → proceed to Stage 2 (seller onboarding). Or it is not → stop, refund the remaining capital, and write down why. **Stopping at day 90 is a successful outcome if the numbers say so**, and it costs a fraction of the alternative.

### Kill criteria (agree these in writing, before day 1)

1. Contribution margin per order below zero for two consecutive months.
2. Failed delivery / return rate above **8%** of orders.
3. AOV still below **P250** after bundling work is exhausted.
4. Fewer than **8 orders/day** by day 45.
5. Orders still being re-keyed by hand after month 2 (means the operating system does not exist).

## 7. Where the moat actually lives

The brief lists the technology stack as infrastructure. In a 2.4-million-person market, software is
not the moat — anyone can buy the same stack. The moat is:

1. **Route density.** A batched delivery network that costs P18 a drop instead of P70 cannot be copied by a competitor who has not spent 18 months building it. This is the real asset.
2. **Category exclusivity.** If we are *the* place to buy hair and baby products online, suppliers must list with us. Depth in one category beats breadth across twenty.
3. **The returns promise.** A trust asset that compounds slowly and is very hard to copy by anyone without local presence — which is exactly why it beats Shein and Temu.
4. **Working capital and supplier terms.** Being able to buy a container at 60-day terms while a competitor pays cash is an unglamorous, decisive advantage.
5. **Operating data.** What sells in Maun in December, which route fails on Fridays, which SKU has an 11% return rate. This is the asset that makes the AI pillar real rather than decorative.

And the honest version of the empire thesis: **the empire is not the storefront, it is the rails.**
Amazon's most durable business is not Amazon.com — it is FBA: other people's inventory moving through
Amazon's logistics, for a fee. The equivalent question for us is:

> **Once Afrikart's delivery and payments work, who else pays to use them?**

Other retailers who cannot afford their own last mile. Suppliers distributing to spazas and salons.
Businesses running B2B procurement. That is the path from a Botswana shop to commerce infrastructure,
and it is measurable — every month we should be able to say whether a third party would pay for our
network yet.

## 8. Where I would still push back on the brief

| Brief says | My concern |
|---|---|
| "Seven interconnected pillars" | Correct as a 10-year org chart. Committing to them now is how focus dies. Pick two for 18 months: **marketplace + logistics**. Everything else is a Phase-4 decision. |
| "Don't attach deadlines to stages" | Agreed on *time*, but each stage still needs a **numeric gate** or it becomes ambition without accountability. |
| "B2B commerce as Pillar 5" | Possibly the strongest pillar in a small market — B2B baskets are P2,000–P20,000 vs P380, with predictable reorder cycles and no cash-on-delivery problem. It may deserve to be **Pillar 2**. |
| "AI engine from the beginning" | Right instinct, wrong first build. At 20 orders/day, AI's job is **delivery exception handling and demand forecasting**, not chatbots and agents. Automate the manual re-keying, then the recommendations. |
| "Cross-border into South Africa" | Reconsider. SA is Takealot's home market with the continent's toughest retail competition. **Namibia** (same customs union, sparse retail, similar income profile) is the better second market. |
| "142,721 e-commerce users" | This is a small number, and it is the whole addressable base today. It argues for **depth in a narrow category**, not breadth across many. |

## 9. Open questions I cannot answer from here

These require fieldwork, not analysis, and each one can change the plan:

1. What is the real landed cost (including duty, freight, forex) of our top 30 SKUs versus the shelf price at Game/Choppies?
2. What is the true first-attempt delivery success rate in Gaborone, by suburb, for cash-on-delivery?
3. What do suppliers currently pay to get goods to customers, and would they pay us to do it?
4. Can we get 30-day supplier terms in the launch category, or is it cash-upfront only? (This decides working capital.)
5. Which payment provider can settle to us fastest, and what is the real effective fee on mobile money vs card?
6. What does a spaza, salon or tuckshop actually reorder weekly, and at what basket size?

To be confirmed with the relevant authority before we take a single Pula — this list is a prompt for

|---|---|
